# Grill with me

An interview that finds the story before any slide exists. Most weak decks fail here: the author starts drawing slides about a topic, and a topic is not a story. The grill ends when you can write the Story Brief below without guessing.

## How to run it

- **Read first.** Open every document, spreadsheet, earlier deck and note the user gave you. Never ask for something you can find. Start the interview by saying what you already understood, in three or four lines, so the user corrects you instead of repeating themselves.
- **One question at a time.** A list of ten questions gets ten thin answers. One question gets a real one.
- **Offer your best guess with each question.** "I think the people in the room are the finance committee, and what they fear is a cost that keeps growing. Is that right?" The user can say yes, or fix one word. A bare question makes them do all the work.
- **Follow the dependencies.** Audience decides everything after it, so it comes first. Then the decision, then the one sentence. Do not ask about colours before you know what the talk is for.
- **Push on vague answers.** The answers that matter most arrive vague the first time. The table below shows the usual ones and what to ask next.
- **Say no when it is true.** If the user wants to add a point that does not serve the decision, say so and offer the appendix.
- **Stop when the brief is written.** Usually 8 to 14 questions. If the user says "just build it", write the brief from what you have, mark each guessed line `ASSUMED`, show it, and continue. An assumption the user can see is fine. A hidden one is not.

If your environment has a tool for asking multiple-choice questions, use it for questions with a few clear answers, with your recommendation first. Use plain text for the open ones.

## The Story Brief

Eight lines. The interview exists to fill them.

```
STORY BRIEF
1. Audience        who decides, what they believe now, what they are afraid of
2. Decision        what they should do after the talk: one verb, one object
3. One sentence    the claim to remember if they forget everything else
4. Why now         what makes waiting costly, starting with forces outside the organisation
5. Proof           the three strongest pieces of evidence, each with its source
6. Hard questions  the three objections you expect, and the honest answer to each
7. Numbers         which numbers go on screen, where each comes from, which are estimates
8. Constraints     minutes, slide language and spoken language, brand, room, privacy, deadline

Material to show: real things (machines, places, products), companies (logos), a demo, people
Cut list:        true things that do not serve the decision (they go to the appendix or out)
```

## The questions

Ask in this order. Skip a question the material already answers, and say what you took from it.

### 1. Audience

- Who makes the decision in the room? One person, or a group that must agree?
- What do they already believe about this? What did they hear last time?
- What are they afraid of: cost, risk, looking wrong, losing control?
- How expert are they? Would they know the three key terms, or do those need a slide each?

The level of the audience sets how much must be taught. A hard idea needs a concept slide before the slide that uses it.

### 2. Decision

- When the talk ends, what do you want them to do? Approve, choose, fund, start, stop?
- What exactly are they approving: how much, for what, from when?
- If they say yes, what happens on the next working day?

A talk that asks for nothing is a report. Reports are fine, but say so, and then the "decision" is what the audience should understand and repeat to others.

### 3. One sentence

- If they remember one sentence a week later, what is it?
- Say it without the word "and". Which half matters more?
- Would a person outside your field understand it?

This sentence becomes the title slide and returns at the close. Everything else in the deck either supports it or goes.

### 4. Why now

- What happens if nothing is decided for a year? What does that cost?
- What changed outside the organisation that the audience cannot argue with: a price, a rule, a competitor, a technology?
- What changed inside: growth, a failure, a deadline?

Outside forces come first in the deck because nobody in the room has to defend them.

### 5. Proof

- What is the single strongest piece of evidence? Did you measure it yourselves?
- What would a sceptic accept: a test you ran, a quote, a public price list, a reference customer?
- Where is the weakest link in the argument? What do you have for it?

Your own measured result beats any outside report. If there is none yet, the honest deck says "we will test this by" and names the date.

### 6. Hard questions

- What is the first question they will ask? And the one you hope they will not?
- Who in the room loses something if this goes ahead?
- What is true about the alternative you are not recommending?

Each answer becomes a row on the questions page in the appendix. A weakness the presenter names first reads as honesty. One the audience finds reads as a flaw.

### 7. Numbers

- Which three numbers must be on screen?
- For each: where does it come from, on what date, and is it measured, quoted, or estimated?
- When options are compared, are they sized for the same work? What is the basis, in one line?
- Which input would the audience most want to change? That one becomes a slider.

No number goes on a slide without a home in the model or the source list. Say this to the user early. It changes what they send you.

### 8. Constraints

- How many minutes, including questions?
- What language are the slides in, and what language will you speak?
- Is there a brand: logo, colours, typeface?
- Where will it be shown: one big screen, a video call, offline?
- What is confidential? Will the link be public?
- When is it due, and is there a rehearsal before?

Minutes set the slide count. Plan about one minute a slide on average, with a press every 15 to 30 seconds, and keep a fifth of the time for questions.

## Vague answers, and what to ask next

| The user says | Ask |
|---|---|
| "The audience is management." | "Which one person can say yes? What did they say last time this came up?" |
| "I want to inform them." | "After they are informed, what do they do differently?" |
| "The message is that our project is important." | "Important enough to do what, instead of what?" |
| "We have lots of data." | "Which one chart would you show if you could show only one?" |
| "It will save money." | "How much, by when, compared with what, and who checked it?" |
| "Everything is important." | "If I cut the deck to five slides, which five?" |
| "They know the background." | "Would they explain it correctly to a colleague? If not, it gets a slide." |
| "Make it look impressive." | "What should they be able to say after it that they could not say before?" |

## Four tests before you leave the grill

1. **Twenty seconds.** Ask the user to say the one sentence and three reasons aloud. If they cannot, the story is not ready and more slides will not fix it.
2. **Do nothing.** State the cost of waiting a year in one line. If it is small, the "why now" chapter is weak and the talk should say so honestly or find a stronger reason.
3. **The no.** Name what would make this audience refuse. The deck must meet that head on, before the ask.
4. **The cut.** Name two true and interesting things that do not serve the decision. Move them to the appendix now, so they do not grow into slides.

## Then grill the storyboard

After you write the slide list, challenge every row before building:

- What does the audience know after this slide that they did not know before it? If the answer is "nothing new", cut it.
- Can the point be a picture? What is the picture?
- Is the title a claim, and would the audience agree with it by the end of the slide?
- Does every number have a source line?
- Which slide would they miss least? Cut that one too.

Show the user the brief and the slide list together. Ask for one thing: "What is wrong or missing?" Then build.

## A short example

> **Agent:** From the two files you sent, I understand this: Example Foods runs a cold-storage warehouse, the power bill is about $42k a month and rising, and an installer has quoted $720k for solar on the roof. I think the talk is for the board, and the decision is to buy. Is that right?
>
> **User:** Yes, but the board is three people and the CFO decides.
>
> **Agent:** Then the CFO is the audience. My guess is the fear is paying $720k now for a saving that may not come. Right?
>
> **User:** Exactly. Last year a "saving" project did not deliver.
>
> **Agent:** Then the proof matters more than the vision. What can we show that the CFO would accept: our own meter data, the installer's guarantee, a site that already did this?
>
> **User:** We have hourly meter data and the roof survey.
>
> **Agent:** Good, both are ours and measured. One more: the supplier also offered to rent us the panels. Is that a real alternative, or already rejected?
>
> **User:** Real. The CFO likes it because there is no money down.
>
> **Agent:** Then the deck has to compare three options on the same energy, and it must say honestly that renting is cheapest in the first years. I would make the one sentence "The roof can pay the power bill", and admit the rental case on the cost slide. Shall I write the brief?
